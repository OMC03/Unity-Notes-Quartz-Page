## Troubleshooting Tips

**Issues Arise**

As you work through the hands-on activities in this course, you may encounter issues due to the following:

1. You are working on a different version of Unity - Unity is updated continuously. While most upgrades are seamless, there are times where an upgrade introduces a new bug, changes the interface slightly, adds new functionality, and/or modifies the way things work.
    
2. You are working on a different platform - the videos are created with the Windows version of the Unity Editor. You may be using the MacOS or Linux version of the Unity Editor. Fortunately, the editor is nearly identical across platforms. There are a few minor differences in hot keys. For example, the Mac using the Command key for menu shortcuts (such as COMMAND-S = Save) while Windows uses the CTRL key (such as CTRL-S = Save). The Mac and Windows file systems (where things are saved) and desktops are slightly different as well. Overall, these differences are fairly minor and I try to point them out along the way.
    
3. You missed a step or misunderstood a step - there is a lot of information presented in the hands-on projects - both step-by-step instructions and conceptual ideas. I do suggest pausing the video so you can follow along and replaying any parts of the video that you missed or find confusing.
    

**Take Action**

If you encounter a problem with your project, such as you are receiving an error message that I am not receiving or the behavior of your project differs from what is presented, try the following steps:

1. Rewatch the lecture and double-check your work. And note that the issue may have actually come up due to something you missed in a previous step OR a change in Unity itself.
    
2. For common issues across learners, I will post a "Solutions to Common Issues" (reading or lecture) after the lecture where the issue arose. If you see a "Solutions to Common Issues" lecture, check it out.
    
3. Browse or search the Coursera discussion forums. If it is a common issue, someone else probably has encountered it and posted the question and others may have already posted a solution.
    
4. Use Google (or your favorite internet search engine) to search for a solution. Unity has a great community around it. You likely will find answers to issues on the [Unity Community](http://unity3d.com/community) website, [Stack Overflow](http://stackoverflow.com/), or elsewhere on the web.
    
5. If you don't find an answer, post your question on Coursera in the appropriate discussion forum. Our great community of learners are great at helping each other out. I will also try to answer as many questions as I can.

## Working in 3D Space

[Working in 3D Space | Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/learn/game-design-and-development-1/lecture/XvP4J/working-in-3d-space#)
**2D Bit Map Graphics**
- Discreate integer-based (device specific)
- 1 = 1 pixel
- (0,0) is located in the top left
- horizontal pixel position refers to the x-coordinate
- vertically down pixel position refers to the y-coordinate

**2D Cartesian Coordinate System**
- Decimal-based (device independent)
- 1 = 1 meter
- (0,0) is located at the origin
- Up is the positive y direction
- Right is the positive x direction
- Down is the negative y direction
- Left is the negative x direction
- Real number or floating point based number system
- Talks about distance from the origin

**Note:**  Unity uses meters as its form of measurement, (1,2) would be one meter to the right and 2 meters up

**Points vs. Vectors**
- Points = location
	- A = (x , y)
- Vectors = displacement in space
	- D = (x1 , y1) to (x2 , y2)
	- Defined by direction and magnitude 
	- Vector can be defined by its displacement from the origin

**3D Cartesian Coordinate System**
- Defined by (x, y, z)
- This can be remembered by XYZ + RGB

**Understanding Transforms**
1. Position
	- Vector3 (x , y , z)
	- Displacement from origin of world coordinate system
2. Rotation
	- Vector3 (x, y, z) 
	- Degree rotation around each axis
3. Scale
	- Vector3 (x , y , z) 
	- Scale factor on each axis where 1 = 100%


A transform packages position, rotation, and scale together

**Materials**

**Lights**
Here you will adjust the directional light and add light to the sun
[Lights | Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/learn/game-design-and-development-1/lecture/aSPJQ/lights)

1. Illumination
	- Allows users to see the environment
2. Modeling
	- Structural and dimensional qualities (reveal or conceal)
	- Spatial Relationships
	- Establish Time of Day
3. Focus
	- Define contrast in the scene
	- Focus on the players attention

**Purpose Of Lights**
1. Ambient
	- Reflects Light
	- Used for Illumination
2. Directional
	- Sunlight (II Rays)
	- Used for Illumination and Modeling
3. Point
	- Light Bulb
	- used for Illumination and Modeling
4. Spot
	- Flashlight
	- used for Modeling and Focus

**Direct Light:** is emitted hits a surface once and then reflects directly into the camera

**Indirect Light:** is all other light that is ultimately reflected into the camera. This includes light that hits a surface serval times and sky light

**Real-Time Lighting:** Light that is calculated at run time while the game plays

**Baked Lighting:** Lighting that is calculated in advance, within the editor. This is then saved as lighting data which is applied at run time

**Note** 
- Light has no geometry
- Lights are processor intensive, the more you have the more processing power is needed
- Shadows and other lighting effects can also negatively affect performance

- Here you will learn more about Parent - Child relationships as well as getting your planets to rotate
- [Adding Behaviors | Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/learn/game-design-and-development-1/lecture/6oSRV/adding-behaviors)

**Cameras**
Here you will learn how to configure the camera to look at a planet as well as create a mini map
[Cameras | Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/learn/game-design-and-development-1/lecture/K3Zlw/cameras#)

1. Perspective
	- Most like a real camera
	- Used in 1st person and 3rd person games
	- Objects closer to the camera seem larger than ones that are far away
1. Orthographic
	- Unlike the real world the size of all objects stay the same regardless of distance
	- Used for top down and isometric views  on games

**Perspective View Frustum**
- Has a cone shaped frustum
- Near field of view close to the camera
- Wide field of view farther from the camera
- The closer the object the larger it becomes
- Has a Camera will render everything between the near clipping plane and far clipping plane
- Anything out of the clipping planes will not be rendered

**Orthographic View Frustum**
- Has a rectangular shaped frustum
- Objects will appear the same relative size no matter where they are
- Near and Far clipping planes apply in the same way
